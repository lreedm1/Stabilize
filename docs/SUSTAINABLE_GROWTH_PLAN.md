# Stabilize Sustainable Growth Plan

**Status:** working business plan for an early public prototype  
**Planning horizon:** 12 months, with a three-year direction  
**Core rule:** grow only when usefulness, safety, reliability, and economics improve together

This plan is a set of testable hypotheses, not a forecast or a promise. Stabilize is currently a founder-led public prototype. It has a free guest experience, optional account memory, and an early subscription mechanism for additional model choice. It does not yet have clinical validation, a repeatable sales process, independent high-stakes review, or a proven route to recurring revenue.

## 1. Executive summary

Stabilize exists to help an overloaded person protect a neglected need, reduce cognitive load, and choose one safe, reversible next step. The public mission creates real operating obligations: inference, hosting, monitoring, support, accessibility, security, safety review, evaluation, and continued maintenance.

The sustainable model should not depend primarily on charging the person in the most depleted state. The proposed structure is:

1. **A constrained free public core** for short stabilization support and deterministic urgent routing.
2. **An optional individual subscription** for nonessential added value such as better model choice, continuity, saved plans, higher limits, or structured follow-through.
3. **Paid organizational pilots** for implementation, local resource configuration, training, evaluation, support, and service-level expectations without access to individual conversations.
4. **Grants and sponsorships** for free usage, accessibility, independent safety and privacy review, multilingual work, and evaluation that may not have an immediate commercial buyer.

The first-year objective is not maximum traffic. It is to prove four things:

- People understand and use the product for the intended job.
- The product creates measurable, modest value without unacceptable harm.
- The real cost of delivering that value can be bounded.
- At least two payer groups will pay enough to support continued operation.

A reasonable Year 1 outcome is a small base of paying individuals, two or three paid organizational pilots, at least one renewal or expansion, a fixed public-access budget, and enough recurring revenue or committed grant funding to maintain six months of runway. Failure to reach those conditions should trigger a narrower product, a redesigned offer, a pause in growth, or an orderly stop rather than indefinite founder subsidy.

## 2. The current mismatch

The mission is durable and public-facing, but the operating model is still temporary:

- The product can incur variable model costs every time it succeeds at attracting use.
- The founder currently carries product, engineering, safety, deployment, support, and strategy risk.
- The existing paid model-choice feature is hidden and sells an implementation detail rather than a fully validated user outcome.
- There is no repeatable organizational offer, partnership process, or sales pipeline.
- The product has not yet earned strong claims about outcomes.
- Free access is described as a user benefit without a visible explanation of who pays for it.

This creates a predictable failure mode: attention increases usage faster than funding, reliability, review capacity, and support. The correct response is not to remove the mission. It is to build an economic system that protects it.

## 3. Strategic principles

### 3.1 Free access is a budgeted product decision

A useful public core should remain accessible, but it must have:

- per-user and per-network abuse limits;
- a fixed monthly inference ceiling;
- a lower-cost default model when quality remains acceptable;
- graceful degradation when the budget is reached;
- explicit funding from paid margin, grants, or sponsorships.

“Free” should mean the end user does not pay for the core interaction. It should not mean the project pretends the interaction has no cost.

### 3.2 Never monetize urgency

Do not paywall deterministic emergency, medical, shelter, or medication routing. Do not create countdowns, fear-based upgrade prompts, guilt, or artificial scarcity. Paid value should come from convenience, continuity, customization, support, and organizational implementation.

### 3.3 Do not sell conversation data

No advertising model, data brokerage, behavioral targeting, employer surveillance, insurer access, or sale of sensitive conversation content. Organizations should receive only what is necessary for implementation and evaluation, preferably aggregate, privacy-bounded operational measures that cannot be used to reconstruct individual conversations.

### 3.4 Evidence before expansion

Do not treat visits, press, or social attention as impact. Scale only after the product demonstrates:

- understandable positioning;
- user-reported usefulness;
- voluntary return use;
- manageable safety and support burden;
- stable unit costs;
- payer willingness to renew.

### 3.5 Reversible growth

Prefer fixed-scope pilots, month-to-month contractor support, explicit spend caps, and small experiments. Avoid permanent headcount, custom enterprise features, or large paid acquisition commitments before repeatable demand.

## 4. Product and market thesis

### 4.1 The user job

The primary job is not “therapy by AI.” It is:

> When I am overloaded and cannot tell what matters first, help me protect the most important immediate need and identify one manageable next step.

This position is narrower, more defensible, and easier to evaluate than broad mental-health or suicide-prevention claims.

### 4.2 The product wedge

The initial wedge is a low-friction web interaction:

- no account required for a guest chat;
- one clear response rather than an endless feed;
- deterministic routing for selected urgent conditions;
- floor-first attention to basic needs and logistics;
- context-aware follow-up actions;
- optional remembered context after sign-in.

### 4.3 Why a payer might care

Individuals may pay for better continuity and capability. Organizations may pay because they want a low-friction support option that can help people orient, find resources, and take the next practical step before a situation becomes harder and more expensive to manage.

The organizational value proposition must remain modest and measurable. Stabilize should not claim to reduce hospitalization, suicide, absenteeism, or liability without evidence. Early pilots should instead measure outcomes such as:

- successful orientation to a next step;
- use of configured local resources;
- user-reported clarity;
- completion of a practical action;
- reduced staff time for repetitive navigation questions;
- acceptable safety and privacy performance.

## 5. Offer architecture

### 5.1 Public core

**User promise:** Ask for help in an overloaded moment and receive a short, floor-first response and one manageable next step.

**Included:**

- guest access;
- short current-turn support;
- deterministic urgent routing;
- public protocol, safety, privacy, and limits pages;
- cost-controlled default model;
- basic context-aware follow-up buttons.

**Not promised:** unlimited use, clinical care, continuous availability under every demand spike, human monitoring, or guaranteed outcomes.

### 5.2 Stabilize Plus

**Initial offer:** the existing paid model-choice subscription, made visible and explained as an experiment that helps fund the service.

**Potential future value, only after user research:**

- longer or more reliable remembered context;
- saved plans and review dates;
- higher usage limits;
- advanced model access;
- structured weekly check-ins;
- cross-device continuity;
- user-controlled export or deletion tools only if privacy and product value justify them.

**Do not include:** emergency access, safety routing, or any feature that makes a nonpaying user less safe.

### 5.3 Organizational pilot

A fixed-scope, three- to six-month pilot should include:

- discovery and implementation plan;
- a dedicated or co-branded entry point only when appropriate;
- configured local resources and escalation language;
- staff orientation and responsible-use training;
- agreed uptime and support expectations;
- privacy and security documentation;
- aggregate operational reporting without individual chat access;
- baseline and end-of-pilot evaluation;
- a final recommendation to renew, redesign, narrow, or stop.

Avoid custom integrations until the same request appears across multiple paying organizations.

### 5.4 Sponsored public access

Sponsors, foundations, and public-interest funders can underwrite:

- a defined number of free sessions;
- accessibility work;
- multilingual evaluation;
- independent clinical, crisis-response, privacy, and security review;
- verified local resource coverage;
- public reporting on what worked and what failed.

Sponsorship should not influence individual responses or grant access to conversation content.

## 6. Customer segments and order of pursuit

### Segment A: individual supporters and power users

**Why first:** the payment rail already exists, the sales cycle is short, and the experiment can reveal whether users value model choice or continuity.

**Risk:** willingness to pay may be low because the visible benefit is not yet strong enough. Do not interpret low conversion as proof that the mission lacks value; it may indicate the offer is weak.

### Segment B: universities and student-support ecosystems

Potential buyers include student affairs, wellness navigation, disability support, peer-support programs, and innovation funds. The initial use case should be resource navigation and overloaded-moment support, not clinical treatment.

### Segment C: community, housing, and human-service organizations

Potential buyers include supportive housing, community programs, diversion programs, and organizations whose staff repeatedly help people with basic needs and urgent logistics.

### Segment D: public agencies and health organizations

These can become larger payers but have longer procurement, privacy, legal, and integration cycles. Pursue only after smaller pilots establish a repeatable offer and operational competence.

## 7. Pricing hypotheses

These are starting points for discovery, not fixed prices.

| Offer | Initial hypothesis | What the price must cover | Decision rule |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Stabilize Plus | **$8–$12 per month**; test the current price before changing it | paid-model inference, payment fees, support, free-core subsidy, continued development | Keep only if users understand the value, paid gross margin is at least 65%, and three-month retention is meaningful |
| Small organizational pilot | **$5,000–$15,000** for 3–6 months | discovery, configuration, training, support, evaluation, risk, and founder time | Do not discount below the full cost of delivery; require a named owner and evaluation plan |
| Annual organization plan after proof | **$15,000–$50,000+** depending on users, support, and configuration | recurring service, support, reporting, review, and public-access contribution | Offer only after at least one pilot renews and implementation is repeatable |
| Sponsored access | **$500–$10,000+** | a defined free-use pool or named public-interest workstream | Report what the funding enabled without exposing user data |

The project should avoid lifetime plans. Variable AI cost and safety obligations make permanent one-time pricing structurally risky.

## 8. Unit economics and financial guardrails

### 8.1 Track the real cost per useful session

For each month, calculate:

```text
model cost
+ Cloudflare and storage cost
+ payment processing
+ support and incident-review time
+ allocated safety/privacy/security review
= total delivery cost
```

Then divide by:

- completed sessions;
- sessions rated useful;
- paying users;
- organizational pilot users.

A cheap response that is not useful is not efficient. A useful response whose support and risk costs are ignored is not profitable.

### 8.2 Paid gross margin target

Target at least **65% gross margin** on the individual paid plan after model and payment costs. A lower margin can be tolerated temporarily during validation, but the plan should not scale if each new paid user increases losses.

### 8.3 Public-access budget

Create a fixed monthly free-use pool. Increase it only when one of these occurs:

- recurring paid gross profit increases;
- a grant or sponsor explicitly funds public access;
- a lower-cost model passes quality and safety evaluation;
- retention or user value improves enough to justify the additional spend.

An initial guardrail is to keep free inference and hosting below **30–40% of dependable recurring revenue plus restricted access funding**. The exact percentage should change with observed costs and mission priorities.

### 8.4 Runway

Maintain at least six months of committed operating expenses before adding permanent staff. Treat unpaid founder labor as a real cost in pricing even when it is not yet paid in cash.

### 8.5 Acquisition

Use no paid advertising during the first validation phase. Growth should come from:

- direct user sharing;
- relevant communities;
- founder outreach;
- pilot partners;
- trusted organizations;
- earned coverage only when the product is ready for demand.

Paid acquisition begins only after activation, retention, unit costs, and the paid offer are understood.

## 9. Go-to-market plan

### 9.1 Individual channel

1. Make the sustainability and paid-plan explanation visible from the main menu and About page.
2. Present the paid offer after value has been demonstrated, not before the user can access the public core.
3. Interview at least 15 paying users and 20 users who decline.
4. Test whether users value model choice, continuity, higher limits, or another outcome more strongly.
5. Keep one paid offer at a time so results are interpretable.

### 9.2 Organizational channel

1. Build a one-page pilot brief with problem, product boundary, privacy model, pilot scope, measures, price range, and stop conditions.
2. Start with 20 high-fit conversations, not mass outreach.
3. Seek two design partners willing to pay at least a meaningful portion of full delivery cost.
4. Use a standard pilot agreement and implementation checklist.
5. Produce one honest case study that includes limitations and negative findings.
6. Ask for renewal or expansion only after the evaluation is complete.

### 9.3 Grant and sponsor channel

Create a funding menu rather than a generic donation request:

- fund 10,000 public chats;
- fund independent safety review;
- fund accessibility remediation;
- fund one local resource implementation;
- fund a multilingual evaluation cohort.

Each funding unit should have a budget, deliverable, and public-safe report.

## 10. Twelve-month operating plan

### Phase 1: foundation and measurement — Months 0–3

**Build:**

- visible About and sustainability pages;
- cost, latency, error, and route measurement without prompt-body analytics;
- rate limiting and monthly spend caps;
- a simple user-reported usefulness and next-step measure;
- independent safety, privacy, and security review plan;
- a defined paid-plan experiment;
- an organizational pilot brief.

**Targets:**

- at least 200 completed real-world sessions;
- at least 30 short user interviews or structured feedback conversations;
- known cost per completed and useful session;
- a documented top-ten failure-mode list;
- no unresolved critical security or safety issue before promotion.

**Exit criterion:** the product is understandable, cost-bounded, and safe enough for a small controlled growth test.

### Phase 2: individual revenue validation — Months 3–6

**Run:**

- one clearly explained paid offer;
- pricing and value interviews;
- onboarding and cancellation review;
- monthly cohort retention analysis;
- support-burden tracking.

**Planning targets, not forecasts:**

- 25–50 paying users;
- paid gross margin at or moving toward 65%;
- at least 40% of a paid cohort still active at three months, or strong evidence that the offer should be redesigned;
- one clear reason users pay that is stronger than “support the founder.”

**Exit criterion:** either a defensible individual offer exists or the project explicitly deprioritizes consumer subscription revenue.

### Phase 3: organizational pilots — Months 6–9

**Run:**

- two or three paid pilots;
- standard privacy and implementation terms;
- baseline and end measures;
- scheduled operational reviews;
- a documented support-hour budget.

**Targets:**

- at least two organizations pay real money rather than only express interest;
- delivery stays within scope;
- at least one pilot produces measurable operational or user value;
- no organization receives access to individual chat content;
- one organization indicates credible renewal intent.

**Exit criterion:** the organizational offer is repeatable enough to price annually, or it is narrowed/stopped.

### Phase 4: deliberate scale decision — Months 9–12

Choose among four routes:

1. **Scale cautiously:** recurring revenue, acceptable incidents, and renewal justify more access and one carefully selected hire or contractor.
2. **Stay small and sustainable:** maintain a public tool plus a few partners without pursuing venture-scale growth.
3. **Narrow the use case:** focus on the strongest segment or job.
4. **Pause or stop:** preserve the protocol and lessons if the product remains unsafe, uneconomic, or unwanted.

The default is not automatic expansion.

## 11. Measurement framework

### User value

- Did the response make the situation clearer?
- Did the user identify a next step?
- Was the step completed or attempted?
- Did the user return voluntarily?
- Did the user understand the product’s limits?

### Safety and quality

- urgent-route accuracy on test sets;
- harmful or materially misleading responses;
- failed or inappropriate human handoffs;
- complaints and incident reviews;
- model regressions after updates;
- accessibility failures;
- privacy or security events.

### Reliability

- uptime;
- response latency;
- error rate;
- cost spikes;
- deployment rollback time;
- support response time for paying organizations.

### Business

- free and paid cost per useful session;
- paid conversion and cancellation;
- three- and six-month retention;
- paid gross margin;
- pilot sales cycle and delivery hours;
- renewal and expansion;
- unrestricted cash runway.

Do not report “lives saved.” The product cannot establish that counterfactual.

## 12. Team and governance

The founder can continue to lead product and engineering during validation, but the following functions require independent input:

- crisis-response and clinical safety review;
- privacy and security review;
- legal review of claims, terms, organizational pilots, and data practices;
- accessibility testing;
- user research with people outside the founder’s network;
- financial bookkeeping and contract administration.

Use contractors or advisors before hiring. Document who can pause a deployment or public campaign when a material risk appears. The founder should not be the only person capable of operating, reviewing, or shutting down the service.

## 14. Main risks and stop conditions

### Risk: harmful or overconfident advice

**Response:** deterministic routing, narrow claims, adversarial tests, independent review, incident process, rollback capability.

**Stop condition:** unresolved severe incident, repeated harmful failure after proportionate redesign, or inability to monitor and respond safely.

### Risk: mission drift toward engagement or surveillance

**Response:** no ads, no sale of conversation data, no organization access to individual chats, no retention metric used without a user-value measure.

**Stop condition:** revenue requires behavior that materially undermines privacy, agency, or safety.

### Risk: variable model costs overwhelm funding

**Response:** spend caps, rate limits, model routing, free-use budget, paid cross-subsidy, sponsor-funded pools.

**Stop condition:** no path to positive paid contribution margin after two pricing or product iterations.

### Risk: no payer values the offer

**Response:** interview nonbuyers, test one value proposition at a time, shift from model choice to a validated continuity or organizational outcome.

**Stop condition:** fewer than two paid pilots or no credible paid retention after a defined 12-month validation period, absent new evidence.

### Risk: founder dependency

**Response:** runbooks, automated tests, documented deployment, contractors, financial reserve, clear ownership.

**Stop condition:** the project cannot operate safely without continuous founder availability.

### Risk: premature clinical positioning

**Response:** remain a support and navigation product unless evidence, governance, legal review, and clinical operations justify a different category.

**Stop condition:** a partner requires claims or workflows the product cannot responsibly support.

## 15. Three-year direction

### Year 1: prove the system

- validate the free-core job;
- identify one paid individual value proposition;
- complete two or three paid pilots;
- obtain one renewal;
- establish independent review and cost controls;
- reach a small but dependable recurring-revenue base.

### Year 2: make delivery repeatable

- standardize implementation and evaluation;
- serve approximately 5–10 paying organizations only if support remains manageable;
- grow the individual paid base through demonstrated value rather than paid traffic;
- publish honest evaluation findings;
- hire selectively only with six months of runway.

### Year 3: choose the appropriate scale

Possible outcomes include:

- a sustainable small public-benefit company;
- a larger software and services business serving public systems;
- a nonprofit or hybrid structure supported by contracts and grants;
- licensing the protocol or infrastructure to trusted operators;
- keeping the product intentionally small if that best protects quality and mission.

The correct structure should follow observed economics and governance needs, not founder identity or startup convention.
